## What we read

Alissa Cooper, Hannes Tschofenig, Bernard Aboba, Jon Peterson, Jon Morris, Marit Hansen, Rhys Smith — **"Privacy Considerations for Internet Protocols"**, IETF RFC 6973 (Informational, July 2013, ISSN 2070-1721) — read the full 48 pp. canonical text at:

- <https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6973.txt> (canonical text, fetched 200 2026-11-02, ETag W/"84fe41ba822834d73fac23bfce4f1eec", 89198 bytes, 2019 lines, PDF rendition at <https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6973.pdf> same bytes)
- <https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc6973/> (IETF datatracker landing, IAB consensus, not Standards-Track)
- Companion re-read for the host-radio boundary only: RFC 9797 (RCM, 2025-06) and IEEE 802.11bh-2024, previously landed for sensing-and-privacy--recent, not re-read as primary here.

Four passages carry the transferable mechanism; each replaces a vague "privacy" claim with a typed checklist this plant can import as pure vocabulary.

1. **Entities and the communications model (§3.1, §4, pp.7–12).** Initiator/recipient/intermediary/enabler/observer/attacker/eavesdropper are defined with "$" tags for automated search (§3). The initiator "acts on behalf of an individual (or different individuals at different times). It is this individual whose privacy is potentially threatened" (§4 p.10). Intermediaries and enablers are "at least tacitly authorized" yet are observers that "may all pose privacy threats … because they are able to observe, collect, process, and transfer privacy-relevant data" (§4 p.11). Role depends on phase: an entity is enabler in one phase and recipient in the next. The guidance asks designers to consider how a protocol "is expected to interact with systems and information that exist outside the protocol bounds" (§2 p.5) without imagining every deployment.

2. **Privacy threats as a checklist separate from security (§5, pp.12–18, citing Solove and CoE).** §5.1 combined threats: surveillance (5.1.1 — even encrypted traffic leaks type/timing via traffic analysis), stored data compromise (5.1.2 — logging/key-management retention must be weighed), intrusion, misattribution. §5.2 privacy-specific: correlation (5.2.1 — "persistent or infrequently replaced identifiers at any layer … allow … activities to be tracked and combined over time," with TLS session_id/ticket linking example p.15), identification (5.2.2 — direct via SIP/XMPP identifiers or indirect via log correlation), secondary use (5.2.3 — authentication server reusing Access-Requests to track location), disclosure (5.2.4 — via RFC 6280 geolocation architecture), exclusion (5.2.5 — failure to let individuals know/participate, e.g. presence authorization rules RFC 5025). Each threat notes who can mount it (often "any observer or attacker," with different exposure per phase).

3. **Mitigations as engineering choices (§6, pp.18–23).** Three classes: data minimization (6.1 — collection limitation plus identifiability reduction via anonymity/pseudonymity/identity confidentiality/data minimization within identity management; "limiting the data collected … to only what is necessary … is the most straightforward way" p.18; periodic creation of new/randomized identifiers reduces correlation p.19), user participation (6.2), security (6.3). Anonymity set (§3.3): "set of individuals that have the same attributes, making them indistinguishable … from the perspective of a particular attacker or observer." Pseudonymity and unlinkability/undetectability are defined as mitigations, not as status claims. The RFC stresses privacy properties often depend on "complete system design … implementation … operational deployment practices, including default privacy settings" (§2 p.5) — i.e. no single protocol verb gives privacy.

4. **Guidelines and a worked example (§7–8, pp.23–31).** §7 asks whether a document needs a standalone privacy considerations section, a security subsection, or treatment throughout — decision depends on content, not a mandate for every RFC (example list: RFC 3325 needs no separate section, RFC 6716 needs none). §8 demonstrates the analysis on an IETF protocol, showing how to enumerate threats, map them to the communications-model entities, and record mitigations as a checkable section rather than prose. The point is that privacy analysis is a thought process (§1 p.4: "guidance … can and should be used to assess … and to decide whether those considerations are to be documented") transplanted from Fair Information Practices/OECD and Privacy by Design [FIPs][OECD][PbD] — the transfer is the mechanism's own history, applied once more onto this plant.

**Limits the source states, kept here.** RFC 6973 is Informational, not Standards-Track; it provides no risk thresholds, no mandatory rotation interval, no wire-format privacy field, and no claim that a considerations section alone satisfies privacy. It assumes recipients/intermediaries are not compromised for security analysis (RFC 3552 §3) but explicitly asks privacy analysis to question that assumption (§4 p.11). "Whether any individual document warrants a specific privacy considerations section will depend on the document's content" (Abstract) — the check is proportional. Eavesdroppers "by their very nature" exclude (p.17), and secondary-use protection is "typically outside the scope of IETF protocols" (p.17).

**Why this literature, for this cell.** The field `sensing-and-privacy` names "host radio (host_radio.py), body perception, personal analysis" and the angle is *transfer* — a cross-domain transfer onto one of this plant's parts. RFC 6973's explicit project is cross-domain transfer of privacy engineering (FIPs/OECD/PbD → IETF protocol design) and it supplies the only IETF-wide privacy-threat checklist that the prior transfers (Kaye EJHG 2015 dynamic consent, the RCM/802.11bh negatives) do not. Reading it as the primary source makes the plant's host-state decoder reviewable under its own "Privacy Considerations" section, parallel to how an RFC would be.

## What this plant already does

*The plant decodes only its host's own adapter state, publishes no OTA peer identifiers, and already carries two negative capabilities — but its privacy boundary is stated as per-sense refusals, not as a typed RFC 6973 considerations section that names threats, entities and mitigations.*

- **Host-state decoding is host-local and negative.** `src/mishe_tauftauf/host_radio.py:1` "Decode this host's radio, wired-network and tailnet adapter state … never mutates a radio"; `BLUETOOTH_ROOT`/`RFKILL_ROOT`/`NET_ROOT` (`:49–51`) plus `bluetooth_controllers`/`wireless_interfaces`/`ethernet_interfaces`/`rfkill_verdict`/`controller_state` (`bluetoothctl show` → Powered/ActiveInstances/SupportedInstances, 10 s timeout) and `docs/protocol-boundaries.md:67–71` ("never mutates a radio … missing adapter … stays UNKNOWN"). Two additive negatives already landed: `WLAN_SENSING_SUPPORTED=False` + `wlan_sensing_capability()` (sensing-and-privacy--recent 802.11bf) and `RCM_LINKING_SUPPORTED=False` + `rcm_linking_capability()`/`is_rcm_linking_free()` citing RFC 9797 §§1–4/6.1, IEEE 802.11bh, 802.11 U/L=1, and 802.1X/AAA trust degrees (Full/Selective/Zero). Both are pure predicates with no I/O on import and no new `sense.*` verdict word.

- **No persistent peer identifiers are read or stored.** `host_radio.py` imports only `re`, `shutil`, `subprocess`, `Path`; it never opens a raw/802.11 socket, never runs `iw`/`tcpdump`, never parses probe requests or sequence numbers, never reads ARP/NDP neighbour tables, never stores a peer MAC/BSSID, never links two U/L=1 addresses to one device (`rg -n "RCM|802\.11bh|U/L" src/mishe_tauftauf/host_radio.py` shows only the RCM negative module doc before this review). This matches RFC 6973's data-minimization and anti-correlation guidance, but the match is implicit — a reviewer must read all function bodies, not one checklist.

- **The private-body sense is the complementary boundary.** `src/mishe_tauftauf/body_perception.py:55` `PERSONAL_CONTEXTS`/`BODY_INFORMATION_TYPES`/`TRANSMISSION_PRINCIPLES`/`_APPROPRIATENESS`/`_CI_FLOWS` (Nissenbaum 2004 + Barth et al. 2006, hardened per Diamantis BEQ 2025), plus `CONSENT_RECEIPT_VERSION`/`PERSONAL_CONSENT_RECEIPTS` (Kaye EJHG 2015 sticky policies, per-type revisable receipts), plus `BELMONT_*` (1979 practice vs research, respect/beneficence/justice), plus `QI_DEFAULT_KEYS`/`K_ANONYMITY_*` (Samarati & Sweeney 1998 Def.2.1/2.3, distance vector/completeness), plus `stats_boundary.py` DP boundary (Dwork & Roth). None of these names RFC 6973's observer taxonomy or its five privacy-specific threats; `rg -n "6973|Privacy Considerations|surveillance|correlation|secondary.use|exclusion" src/` is 0 hits pre-review (only crowded-memory fixture noise matching "surveillance" as substring, not in `src/`).

- **The broadcast channel is the residual RFC 6973 surface.** `outcome_publication.py` + `docs/how-it-works.md:239` + `docs/protocol-boundaries.md:224` publish every wall outcome/DM/discovery event to every attached mind as a single distribution. That distribution is the plant's analogue of RFC 6973's "observer" and "secondary use / disclosure / stored data compromise" surface: any `sense.body.redmi10` field quoted into a wall outcome becomes visible to all recipients/intermediaries on the bus. The current protection is the typed CI/Belmont/receipt denials plus the dense-queue k-anonymity/DP measurement (body_perception.py:1021 DENSE_TAIL_BYTES etc.), not a named data-minimization/participation checklist for the publication path.

*Consequence the RFC predicts.* Without an explicit Privacy Considerations section, a future contributor can add a new `sense.*` word or a new `host_radio.py` helper that looks benign on its own (like TLS session resumption looked benign before correlation was named) and combine with existing buses/logs to create correlation, identification or secondary use that no single file's docstring warns about. §2 p.5: "An individual protocol may be relatively benign on its own … But when deployed within a larger system … its use may create new privacy risks."

## What is new here

**Privacy Considerations as a typed, additive checklist beside the adapter decoder — so a new sensing contribution fails the checklist review rather than being judged by per-sense prose.**

Three ideas transfer directly, each with a line number where it would land and each bounded by what the source concedes.

1. **Threat vocabulary as a frozen set (§5, pp.12–18).** `RFC6973_PRIVACY_THREATS: Final[frozenset[str]] = frozenset({"surveillance","stored_data_compromise","intrusion","misattribution","correlation","identification","secondary_use","disclosure","exclusion"})` with module-doc citation of the exact RFC section per term. This does not claim the threats are present; it makes them nameable so a reviewer can ask "which of these does this new helper enable?" The plant's existing vocabulary never names secondary use or exclusion — the two threats most relevant to a wall broadcast.

2. **Entity/role vocabulary and the communications-model mapping (§3.1, §4, pp.7–12).** `RFC6973_OBSERVER_ROLES: Final[frozenset[str]] = frozenset({"initiator","recipient","intermediary","enabler","observer","attacker","eavesdropper"})` plus docstring mapping: this host's decoder is initiator acting on behalf of the operator (the individual, §4 p.10); wall subscribers are recipients; delivery buses are intermediaries/enablers; any mind that logs `sense.body.redmi10` is an observer that may correlate. This makes the "who can see a reading" boundary the same question RFC 9797 answered for OTA/OTWi with MACs, but for the wall bus. The RFC's nuance that eavesdroppers exclude by nature and that intermediaries are "at least tacitly authorized" (§4 p.11) is kept verbatim so the transfer does not claim intermediaries are attackers.

3. **Mitigation checklist as data-minimization and participation facts (§6–7, pp.18–26).** `RFC6973_MITIGATIONS: Final[dict[str, str]]` mapping each threat to the plant's current mitigation note (e.g. `correlation: "no persistent peer identifiers; host adapters only; no peer MAC store; tailnet peer hostname constant only"`; `secondary_use: "wall broadcast is the disclosure path — body fields require CI/Belmont/receipt allow and are not public-by-default"`). Plus two pure predicates with no I/O: `host_radio_privacy_considerations() -> dict` (returns the frozen checklist for review tooling) and `is_host_radio_data_minimized() -> bool` (true while the module satisfies 6.1 collection limitation + no-identifiers: reads only sysfs/`bluetoothctl show`, no content, no peer IDs). The predicates publish no new `sense.*` verdict and add no file; they are import-safe facts that the protocol-boundaries reviewer can cite. Homomorphic encryption, Tor, presence rules, or a new verdict word are not transferred — only the checklist that makes the next sensing PR answer §7's question explicitly.

**Limits the proposal keeps from the source.** No new sensor, no precise location/microphone/camera/biometrics read, no change to any `sense.*` state word or freshness gate, no Homomorphic/RFC 6280/5025 wire format, no claim that a considerations section alone satisfies privacy (§2 system+implementation+deployment factors remain). The "whether a document warrants a section" proportionality (§1 p.4) is kept: host_radio warrants one because it is the plant's radio-adjacent decoder; a codec module would not.

3. **Document the checklist contract in the module docstring and per-symbol docstrings.** The header that already cites RFC 9797 §§1–4/6.1 and IEEE 802.11bh/802.1E gains sentences citing RFC 6973 §§3–7: the threat list (§5), entity model (§3.1+§4), anonymity set (§3.3 p.8), data-minimization/participation/security classes (§6), and the proportionality guideline (§7) with the §2 caveat that privacy depends on system+implementation+deployment. Each symbol docstring reproduces the exact RFC line that defines it so a reviewer sees the checklist is the RFC's, not a rephrasing.
