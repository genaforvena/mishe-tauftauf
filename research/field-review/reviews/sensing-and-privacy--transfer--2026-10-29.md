## What we read

Jane Kaye, Edgar A. Whitley, David Lund, Michael Morrison, Harriet Teare and Karen Melham — **"Dynamic consent: a patient interface for twenty-first century research networks"**, *European Journal of Human Genetics* 23, 141–146 (2015), doi:10.1038/ejhg.2014.71 — read full 6 pp. PDF at:

- <https://doi.org/10.1038/ejhg.2014.71> (canonical DOI, published online 7 May 2014, issue 2015)
- <https://www.nature.com/articles/ejhg201471> (Nature EJHG landing page, same paper, 200 redirect to article; PDF fetched 200 2026-10-29 at <https://www.nature.com/articles/ejhg201471.pdf>, 258 KB, pdftotext 693 lines, title "Dynamic consent: a patient interface for twenty-first century research networks")
- <https://www.nature.com/articles/ejhg201471.pdf> (direct PDF, verified 200, ETag match)

Four passages carry the transferable mechanism, each with the plant-level analogue it replaces.

1. **Dynamic consent as ongoing interface, not static form (Abstract; p.141 Box 1, p.142 "What is dynamic consent?").** "Dynamic Consent is a new approach for engaging individuals about the use of their personal information. It is also an interactive personalised interface that allows participants to engage as much or as little as they choose and to alter their consent choices in real time." (Box 1) "The expression of individual autonomy is also not static: it involves making choices and decisions … over the course of one's lifetime. … current guidelines already recognise consent as a process; an ongoing interaction between researcher and participant. The paper-based tools … have, however, limited ongoing engagement." (p.142) The paper explicitly positions paper consent as "locked in time to the beginning of the research process" (p.143).

2. **Preferences travel with the data as machine-readable wrapper — "sticky policies" + encrypted processing (p.143).** "Reliable storage and enforcement of consent choices is achieved by electronically and cryptographically ‘wrapping’ the individual's consent preferences with samples/information provided. This is possible because of machine-readable disclosure policies or ‘sticky policies’ that attach to data. This package of ‘wrapped information’, which contains specific consent provisions, travels with a participant or donor's data and samples as these are shared or accessed for different purposes. ‘Wrapped Information’ embraces new homomorphic encryption techniques, which allow information to be processed in its encrypted state…" (p.143). The scope travels, not just the bytes.

3. **Granular, revisable choices per study with fatigue avoidance — not replacement for broad consent (p.143).** "Depending upon the nature of the research enterprise, participants could consent to a broad range of uses … or opt to be approached on a case-by-case basis, or set different preferences for different types of research. These preferences can be ‘opt ins’ or ‘opt outs’…", and: "Dynamic consent is not a replacement for existing models such as broad consent but rather a facilitation tool to improve how that consent is obtained, understood and acted upon. The ‘opt in’ and ‘opt out’ approach … means that a participant can still choose to give a broad consent and not receive updates … but if at some future point they wish to become more engaged they have the option to do so." The anticipated critique — "consent fatigue" where participants are asked "over and over again" (p.143, citing p.26) — is answered by tailoring, not by abandoning granularity.

4. **One accessible record of all transactions, tailored to participant and research enterprise (p.143–144 Boxes 2–3).** Box 2: "It provides a record of all transactions and interactions in one place. It allows people to be approached for different kinds of consent … Consent preferences can be modified over time." Box 3 lists participant capabilities: "Consent to new projects … or alter their consent preferences in real time … Change contact information and personal preferences and find out how their samples have been used via a secure, password-protected section … Set preferences about the kind of information they receive, how often … and in what format." Design constraints handled: digital divide (§p.143, "so-called ‘digital divide’ … tailored to increase inclusiveness" via tablet/mobile/carer delegation), configurable per biobank/cohort/LIMS (p.144), and cost/complexity offsets versus recruitment/trust gains.

**Limits the source states, kept here.** Dynamic consent was developed for biobanking research networks, not for device telemetry; the WRAP/sticky-policy + homomorphic encryption architecture is described as an available capability (§p.143 refs 30–31), not as deployed infrastructure — "new homomorphic encryption techniques" are future-facing. The paper does not prescribe a threshold for re-contact frequency, does not claim broad consent is unlawful (it questions whether it "will remain a lawful option" under proposed EU GDPR explicit-consent, p.142), and notes ongoing governance mechanisms (UK Biobank, Norwegian Mother Child Cohort) already represent transitional broad-but-updated consent (p.142). Costs, learning time, and content-population effort are explicit ("financial costs … work associated with understanding, specifying and implementing specific consent options", p.144). The transfer keeps these boundaries: wrapper-travel and per-type revisability transfer; homomorphic compute and biobank LIMS wiring do not.

**Why this literature, for this cell.** The field `sensing-and-privacy` names "host radio, body perception, personal analysis" and the angle is *transfer* — a cross-domain transfer onto one of this plant's parts. Kaye et al.'s explicit project is cross-domain transfer of consent technology from human-computer interaction / trusted computing (sticky policies, homomorphic encryption) onto biomedical research governance, so the transfer is the mechanism's own history applied once more, onto `body_perception.py`.

## What this plant already does

*The plant has a single private mandate, closed-world CI vocabulary that denies by default, and no wrapper that travels — exactly the static, paper-locked consent Kaye is written to replace.*

- **One blanket mandate with an explicit future clause, stored once on disk.** `.mishe-tauftauf/research/shared/personal-multibody-analysis-20261015.md:5` records the human's 2026-10-15 consent for analysis "about themselves from authorized sensed evidence" across "all authorized bodies and senses, including new not-yet-developed ones" with the line "This consent concerns the human, not bystanders." It is a single markdown file the operator and doc track, not a per-information-type preference store. There is no file beside it that records *which* information types were consented, *when*, under *which* principle, or a revocation. The CI tables decode this blanket as the single principle `"human-consented-personal-analysis"` confined to non-public recipients (`body_perception.py:71` `("personal-carrying","human-consented-personal-analysis") -> {senses, health, body-research, inference-research}`).

- **Closed-world CI vocabulary that already denies the frog vector, but cannot show the wrapper.** `src/mishe_tauftauf/body_perception.py:33` `PERSONAL_CONTEXTS` / `:59` `BODY_INFORMATION_TYPES` / `:71` `TRANSMISSION_PRINCIPLES` / `:88` `_APPROPRIATENESS` / `:103` `_CI_FLOWS` plus pure predicates `is_body_info_appropriate_for_context` / `contextual_integrity_allows` / `personal_body_flow_allows` are typed, additive, and deny any `(context, info_type)` or `(context, principle, recipient)` not listed (hardened per Diamantis BEQ 2025 §§3.2–3.6). The module header states the blanket's future clause "does not auto-extend the vocabularies" — a new type needs an explicit entry plus revisited consent. This is the correct *refusal* but it keeps consent as external prose the predicate cannot read: `contextual_integrity_allows` checks the table, not a receipt attached to the reading.

- **Telemetry carries sense ID + freshness, not the principle or a receipt.** `sense.body.redmi10` (`body_perception.py:29` `SENSE_ID`, `FRESH_SECONDS=30.0`) publishes `(id, kind, state, freshness)` and the device fields (`battery/light/acceleration incl. gravity` + `wifi_link_rssi_dbm`/`ble_scan` auxiliary). Neither the `(context, principle)` tuple nor any receipt reference is published beside the reading. A later mind quoting `L=105lux |a|=9.84 incl-g` into a wall outcome cannot tell from bytes alone which consent wrapper authorized the quote — the link is `body_perception.py` comments and the shared mandate prose, not typed data.

- **Host-radio has the matching negative boundary already.** `src/mishe_tauftauf/host_radio.py:35` `BLUETOOTH_ROOT`/`RFKILL_ROOT`/`NET_ROOT` plus `WLAN_SENSING_SUPPORTED = False` (sensing-and-privacy--recent review) makes explicit that the plant does NOT perform device-free WLAN sensing; that boundary stays separate from the body-perception consent question.

- **Existing revocable-decision vocabulary elsewhere is not wired to sensing consent.** `src/mishe_tauftauf/access.py:24` `Request`/`Decision` with `decision in {granted, revoked}` and file-backed `access/requests/` already models revocable, scoped permissions for node-local capabilities — but sensing consent is not carried as a `Request` and the CI check does not consult that store. `multibody.py` covers whether windows *exist*, not whether a type *may be collected*.

*Consequence the paper predicts.* A consent that is "locked in time" at recruitment (p.143) plus a repository that can add new body senses (`multibody.py:278` windows, `body_perception:52` "microphone, geolocation, csi_presence" listed as examples not yet in the set) is exactly the dynamic research network the paper describes: re-contact is needed as new projects/senses arise, anonymisation is increasingly testable, and broad consent alone leaves the plant unable to show per-type scope the third-party reader can verify. The current denial-by-default is safe but opaque — safe because unlisted types fail the predicate; opaque because there is no place to *affirm* a type with a dated, per-type consent record that travels.

## What is new here

**Consent that travels with the data as a typed, revisable wrapper — so a reading's authorizing scope is checkable from the store, not only from prose, and a new body sense cannot become authorized by a table edit alone.**

Three ideas transfer directly, each with a line number where it would land and each bounded by what the source concedes.

1. **Sticky policy: the preference wraps the data and travels with it (§p.143 "machine-readable disclosure policies or ‘sticky policies’ that attach to data").** Today the tuple `(context, info_type, principle, recipient)` is implicit in which table row would allow the flow. The transfer makes it explicit as a stored, machine-readable receipt that moves with the reading's bytes: the reading's allowed flows are enumerated in the wrapper, and a consumer that receives the bytes receives the scope. This is not encryption of the sample — homomorphic wrapping (p.143) does not transfer — only the typed scope record that the premise enables. Landing site: a `PERSONAL_CONSENT_RECEIPTS` / `CONSENT_SCOPE` mapping beside `PERSONAL_CONTEXTS`/:33 in `body_perception.py`, additive, with no I/O on import.

2. **Per-type, alterable-in-real-time preferences with a broad-consent option that remains broad-but-revisable (p.143).** The paper answers "consent fatigue" by keeping broad consent as a valid choice *and* making every choice revisable without re-consenting from scratch. For this plant that means: the 2026-10-15 blanket stays valid for the five listed `BODY_INFORMATION_TYPES`, but each type carries its own dated receipt (origin file, principle, allowed recipients, optional expiry) that can be revoked or narrowed without editing `_APPROPRIATENESS`/`_CI_FLOWS`. Adding `microphone` or `geolocation` does not inherit the blanket — it needs a new receipt. This is the direct operationalisation of the critique review's "non-self-executing blanket" line, now typed so a consumer can test it. A new information type without a receipt is denied even if someone adds it to `BODY_INFORMATION_TYPES` — the receipt is the additional check.

3. **One accessible record of all transactions and re-contact preferences (Box 3, p.144).** The biobank interface "provides a record of all transactions … in one place" and lets participants "find out how their samples have been used." The plant analogue is a single markdown/JSON ledger of consent receipts beside the existing mandate that a consumer can read without scanning wall prose. The reading `sense.body.redmi10` does not itself gain a new `SENSE_ID` row — the receipts are authorisation metadata, not sensor state — so no `GREEN`/`RED`/`UNKNOWN` verdict is added. The existing `access.py` grant/revoke pattern is the precedent to reuse (validation idiom, file-backed store), but the receipts live beside the body-perception vocab so `is_body_info_appropriate_for_context` remains a pure table check and the consent check is a second pure check over the receipts.

**Limits the proposal keeps from the source.** No homomorphic encryption, no password-protected web interface, no SMS/email preference channel, no LIMS/wall-protocol wire-format change — only the typed receipt and the two pure predicates that consult it. The paper's cost/tailoring boundary stays: running the interface over tablet/mobile/carer delegation (p.143–144) is not transferred; the digital-divide mitigation here is that receipts are local markdown readable by any mind, not a remote service. The "record of all transactions" is scoped to consent receipts, not to per-sample access logs — the latter would be telemetry logging, which the paper does not specify operationally.

**Make dynamic consent a typed, revisable, travel-ready fact beside the existing Redmi decoders — pure, additive receipts that wrap each body information type, without changing any `sense.*` verdict, without new I/O on import, and without publishing a person claim.**

1. **Add a module-level consent-receipt vocabulary beside `PERSONAL_CONTEXTS` / `BODY_INFORMATION_TYPES` / `TRANSMISSION_PRINCIPLES` in `src/mishe_tauftauf/body_perception.py`.** New symbols (names illustrative, additive only): `CONSENT_RECEIPT_VERSION: Final[str] = "1"` and `PERSONAL_CONSENT_RECEIPTS: Final[dict[str, dict[str, object]]]` mapping each `BODY_INFORMATION_TYPES` member to its receipt `{principle: str, origin: str, date: str, recipients: frozenset[str], scope_note: str}` — initial entries for the five listed types point to `".mishe-tauftauf/research/shared/personal-multibody-analysis-20261015.md"` with `principle="human-consented-personal-analysis"` and `recipients=frozenset({"senses","health","body-research","inference-research"})` (matching `_CI_FLOWS:103`). Docstrings cite Kaye et al. EJHG 2015 Boxes 1–3 and p.143 sticky-policy paragraph and state: consent is an ongoing, per-type preference that can be altered in real time; the blanket mandate does not auto-extend to new types; a type without a receipt is not consented even if it appears in `BODY_INFORMATION_TYPES`; receipts travel with the reading as machine-readable scope (the plant's analogue of "wrapped information" without homomorphic compute). Any new type (`microphone`, `geolocation`, `csi_presence`) requires a new entry *and* a dated, per-type consent line in the shared mandate file.

2. **Add two pure predicates beside `is_body_info_appropriate_for_context` / `contextual_integrity_allows` with no sysfs/daemon I/O.** `has_personal_consent_receipt(info_type: str) -> bool` (receipt exists for this type) and `personal_consent_allows(*, info_type: str, recipient: str, transmission_principle: str) -> bool` (conjunction of: receipt exists, receipt's `principle` equals the requested principle, `recipient` is in receipt's `recipients`, and `is_body_info_appropriate_for_context` + `contextual_integrity_allows` would also allow the flow). Both are pure views over the frozen receipt map; they publish no `GREEN`/`RED`/`UNKNOWN`, add no `SENSE_ID` row, and are callable without `home` or `now`. `personal_body_flow_allows` may be rewritten to delegate to `personal_consent_allows` for the `personal-carrying` context so the consent-receipt becomes the additional gate the sticky policy promises.

3. **Document the travel and revisability contract in the module docstring and per-symbol docstrings.** The header that already cites Nissenbaum WLR 79:119 and Diamantis BEQ 2025 gains one sentence naming Kaye et al. EJHG 2015 dynamic consent (sticky policies, per-type revisability, record of all transactions in one place, Boxes 1–3) as the consent-receipt lens and stating that any redistribution of `sense.body.redmi10` is a privacy-boundary change reviewed under `docs/protocol-boundaries.md` *and* under the per-type receipt store. Each receipt docstring reproduces the paper's "preferences travel with samples/information" line so a reviewer sees the closed world being made typed and revisable.
