## What this plant already does

The plant already runs a WAL. Intents, starts and outcomes are not updates to a record but appends to three separate JSONL files under one fcntl store lock; a COMMIT is the appended outcome. A crash between intent/start and outcome leaves the operation `unknown` for a successor to reconcile, never silently retried — the inversion SQLite describes (new content appended separately from the original, commit as appended record). Writers append only; a single writer holds the lock while readers can re-read journals, and `os.fsync` on each append is the durability primitive SQLite is trying to minimize. The only reclamation the store does is explicit: `drop_reserved` / `_rewrite_without` for a never-dispatched reservation and `_replace_intent` for a reservation upgrade, both via sibling tmp + `os.replace` + fsync, with parsed-prefix invalidation — no automatic checkpoint that compacts completed operations.

## What is new here

The transferable mechanism is SQLite's third primitive. WAL adds `checkpointing` beside reading and writing: "there are now three primitive operations: reading, writing, and checkpointing." By default SQLite checkpoints when the WAL reaches 1000 pages, and the docs warn that this extra operation is something developers must be mindful of, with the extra `-wal`/`-shm` files as a file-format cost.

This plant has the WAL without the checkpoint. The journals retain full history by design: completed operation ids are never pruned, and lookup scans the journals. That is the analogue of a WAL with autocheckpoint disabled. SQLite's discipline is the transfer: bound the WAL explicitly and make its size mindful, rather than letting it grow unbounded or silently truncating it. The concrete corollaries are: (a) a checkpoint that shortened a completed-op history would be a lossy compaction analogous to changing `page_size` in WAL mode (which SQLite forbids), so it must preserve every `operation_id` outcome completeness; (b) "WAL is faster because it uses fewer fsyncs" is already what the plant claims for `os.fsync`-per-append vs flush-only, and the cost side is the same growth-for-durability trade; (c) SQLite's wal-index via shared memory is why WAL "does not work over a network filesystem" — the plant's same-host `fcntl` lock is the same boundary, and no cross-host journal share is implied.

No new durability or concurrency claim is imported. The plant does not claim power-loss durability beyond `fsync` on the host FS, nor does it move to a `DELETE` or `TRUNCATE` journal mode. The gap is observability of the retained WAL.
