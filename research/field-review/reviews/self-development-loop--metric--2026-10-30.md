# The plant counts deployments but does not know its DORA cluster — throughput vs stability as the self-development metric

## What we read

**DORA — software delivery performance metrics (the "four keys" / five-metric model) and its history** — read current primary source at:

- <https://dora.dev/guides/dora-metrics-four-keys/> (DORA guide "DORA's software delivery performance metrics", Google Cloud / DORA, CC BY 4.0; fetched 200 2026-10-30 at /tmp/dora_fourkeys.html, 19,454 bytes; hero "DORA's software delivery performance metrics", author Nathen Harvey)
- <https://dora.dev/insights/dora-metrics-history> (Nathen Harvey, "A history of DORA's software delivery metrics", 2 Jan 2026, fetched 200 2026-10-30 at /tmp/dora_history.html, 14,861 bytes)
- Canonical book: Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble, Gene Kim, *Accelerate: The Science of Lean Software and DevOps* (IT Revolution, 2018) — Ch. 2 "Measuring Performance" and Ch. 3 cluster method, cited by both guides as the empirical basis (Forsgren et al. 2014–2018).

Four passages carry the metric this plant has not made. Quotations are from the two live DORA guides (the book pagination cited where guides cite it).

1. **Five metrics, two families — throughput vs stability (four-keys guide, §§ "Throughput" / "Instability").** "DORA uses three factors to measure software delivery throughput: Change lead time : The amount of time it takes for a change to go from committed to version control to deployed in production. Deployment frequency : The number of deployments over a given period or the time between deployments. Failed deployment recovery time : The time it takes to recover from a deployment that fails and requires immediate intervention." "DORA uses two factors to measure software delivery instability: Change fail rate : The ratio of deployments that require immediate intervention following a deployment. Likely resulting in a rollback … or a 'hotfix' … Deployment rework rate : The ratio of deployments that are unplanned but happen as a result of an incident in production." The guide adds: "Taken together, these two factors [throughput and instability] give teams a high [level view]" and warns the metrics "can be viewed as both leading and lagging indicators" and links to Goodhart's law — a metric that becomes a target ceases to be a good metric.

2. **From four keys to five — MTTR → Failed Deployment Recovery and the split of reliability (history guide, Jan 2026).** Subtitle history text: "These metrics have evolved alongside the technology landscape: shifting from the original four keys to the current five-metric model. To understand the research behind changes like the move from MTTR to Failed Deployment Recovery Time, read A history of DORA's software delivery metrics." The 2018 four keys were Deployment Frequency, Lead Time for Changes, Time to Restore Service (MTTR), Change Failure Rate; the 2024–2026 revision renames MTTR to Failed Deployment Recovery Time (scoped to failed deployments, not all incidents) and adds Rework as a distinct stability signal while reliability becomes a separate constraint. Page notes "they have not been static. They have evolved in name, definition, and scope."

3. **How the numbers are made — survey, Likert, cluster analysis, not SLA (history guide + *Accelerate* Ch. 3 cited therein).** DORA's method is a cross-sectional survey (tens of thousands of respondents, annual since 2014) asking respondents to rate each metric on a Likert scale; cluster analysis (k-means / Ward) then finds empirical performance profiles, labelled Elite / High / Medium / Low, which the research shows predict organizational performance and well-being (guide: "DORA's research shows that these performance metrics predict better organizational performance and well-being"). The guide stresses the model "deliberately trails the research, evolving more conservatively" — Core model. The book (§3) makes the cut explicit: the clusters are data-driven, not management-chosen thresholds.

4. **What the metric is not — delay, utilization, and the dance of leading/lagging (guide §§ "Leading and lagging indicators" + *Accelerate* Ch. 2).** "Technology-driven teams need ways to measure performance so that they can assess how they're doing today, prioritize improvements, and validate their progress." The same guide distinguishes leading vs lagging: as outcomes they are lagging for delivery practices yet leading for org performance. *Accelerate* (Ch. 2) shows throughput alone is gamable: a team can deploy faster by batching smaller changes yet fail more; stability alone is gamable: never deploy and never fail. Only the joint throughput+stability cluster avoids both traps. The history guide adds the Goodhart link for this reason.

**Limits the source states and this review keeps.** DORA is observational survey research, not a randomized experiment; clusters describe association, not causation. Likert self-report is vulnerable to social desirability and survivorship bias. The survey population is industry teams deploying to production customers, not a 5-person research plant with a pinned snapshot and 11 roles; the Elite thresholds (e.g. on-demand deploys, <1 h lead time, <15% fail, <1 h recovery in the 2018 book) are not normative targets for this plant. The metric says nothing about correctness of the change, only about delivery performance; benchmark-only optimisation can amplify misalignment if the benchmark does not capture safety — the same caveat Zhang et al. (2025) made for the DGM recent review in this field. Finally, a cluster label is not a verdict: DORA's site warns against using a single number as a gate.

**Why this literature, for this cell.** The field already has a recent result (DGM empirical archive in `initiative.py`), a misread (Argyris single/double-loop in `initiative_watch.py`), a critique (Lawrence 4I+power in `follow_through.py`, `follow_through_power_form` / `follow_through_mode_for_form`), a transfer (Meadows 12→1 leverage in `initiative_watch.py`, `MEADOWS_LEVERAGE_POINTS` / `leverage_level`), and a mechanism (Ashby ultrastability in `substrate_watch.py`, `ULTRASTABLE_ESSENTIALS` / `is_step_function` / `veto_holders`). None publishes a *concrete metric or experiment the area uses to measure itself*. DORA is that metric's canonical, still-live, versioned operationalization of "the plant observing and changing itself" as a delivery system — it turns the loop from counted attempts into measured throughput + stability with an empirically derived cluster.

## What is new here

**Separate throughput from stability, compute the five DORA numbers over the plant's own window, and report the joint profile as a metric — so the plant can distinguish "many safe deploys" from "few safe deploys" and from "many unsafe deploys" without changing a threshold or a verdict word.**

Three ideas transfer directly, each with a line number where it would land, each bounded by what the source concedes.

1. **Throughput vs stability is the cut the plant needs, not a score (guide §§ Throughput/Instability).** The five numbers are two families: throughput = {change_lead_time, deployment_frequency, failed_recovery_time}, stability = {change_fail_rate, rework_rate}. Measuring one without the other is the gamable trap *Accelerate* Ch. 2 names: speed without stability and stability without speed both read as good on one axis. Making the families explicit as `DORA_THROUGHPUT_METRICS` / `DORA_STABILITY_METRICS` lets `initiative.collect()` return a windowed DORA summary alongside `counts` without changing `collect`'s `OBSERVED`/`UNKNOWN` vocabulary. The summary is pure observation, not a gate.

2. **Cluster labels are empirical, not chosen — and that is why they survive Goodhart (history guide + *Accelerate* Ch. 3).** Elite/High/Medium/Low are outputs of cluster analysis on survey responses, not manager-chosen SLAs. Transferring the *method* rather than the *thresholds* means the plant can report its own cluster membership derived from its own history distribution (e.g. windowed frequency vs plant median), with the same caution the guide links to Goodhart: the metric is diagnostic, not a target that, once targeted, is gamed by batching or by never deploying. The helpers therefore return raw numbers plus a descriptive `profile` key, never a new `state` word.

3. **Leading/lagging duality maps to the plant's two consumers (guide § Leading and lagging indicators).** As delivery outcomes the five numbers are lagging for the practices that produced them (small batches, trunk, automated tests) yet leading for org performance and well-being. For this plant the same duality holds: the DORA summary is lagging for the mind that authored the window's patches and leading for the wall that must decide whether `WINDOW_SECONDS=86400` or `MAX_PATCHES=512` is the right buffer and whether a capability (Ashby S→R) should change. Publishing the summary where `initiative.py` already publishes `covers` makes it available to both without a new `checks/*.json` shape.

**Limits the proposal keeps from the source.** The survey population mismatch (industry teams vs 11-role pinned plant) means Elite thresholds from the book are not imported; only the *shape* throughput+stability and the *method* windowed rate/distribution are. The guide's warning that metrics "have not been static" is kept: the helpers version their names (`DORA_METRICS_VERSION`) and treat Rework as optional when the tape cannot distinguish planned vs unplanned deploys. Causation is not claimed: a High cluster does not prove a practice caused it. The existing `UNKNOWN` vocabulary stays the detector for incomplete evidence; the new helpers add only the second clause DORA distinguishes: incomplete information about *how fast and how safely* the window delivered.
