# The plant needs two feedbacks, not one — Ashby's ultrastable mechanism onto the substrate observer

## What we read

**W. Ross Ashby, *Design for a Brain: The Origin of Adaptive Behaviour*, 2nd ed. revised (London: Chapman & Hall / New York: John Wiley, 1960), Chapters 6–7 (Ultrastability) — read full text at:**

- <https://archive.org/details/designforbrainor00ashb> (Internet Archive landing page; `details/designforbrainor00ashb`)
- <https://archive.org/download/designforbrainor00ashb/designforbrainor00ashb.pdf> (PDF, 18 MB, fetched 200 2026-10-30 at /tmp/ashby_dfb.pdf; `pdfinfo` Title "Design for a brain; the origin of adaptive behavior" / Author "Ashby, William Ross" / 304 pages / CreationDate 2008-07-04 verified; `pdftotext` header "DESIGN FOR A BRAIN / The origin of adaptive behaviour / W. ROSS ASHBY" and "Second Edition / REVISED / 1960" verified; orig. 1st ed. 1952, 2nd ed. 1960)

Four passages carry the operational mechanism this plant has not made. Section numbers are Ashby's `chapter/section` (e.g. S. 6/9 = Ch. 6 §9); page numbers are the PDF pagination of the 1960 printing.

1. **The veto theorem — each part can prevent the whole from being at equilibrium (S. 6/9–6/10, PDF pp. 78–79).** Ashby joins two parts A(u,v) and B(w,x,y) and shows the whole has equilibrium at (7,2,3,1,5) iff A is at equilibrium at (7,2) with parameters (3,1,5) *and* B at (3,1,5) with parameters (7,2). Consequence: "every part has a power of veto over the states of equilibrium of the whole" (p. 78) — a part that is not at equilibrium, though all others are, "will change, will provide new conditions for the other parts, will thus start them moving again" (p. 79). The importance (S. 6/10): "each part acts selectively towards the set of possible equilibria of the whole. Since Chapter 1 we have been looking for some factor that can be both mechanistic and also selective. The next chapter will show this factor in action."

2. **Essential variables are the second input — what threatens the organism if behaviour does nothing (S. 7/3, PDF pp. 81–82; defined S. 3/14–3/15, PDF pp. 68–69).** The diagram of immediate effects becomes environment → essential variables → S → R → environment (Fig. 7/3/1, p. 81: essential variables represented as a dial with limit-marks). Ashby distinguishes R (the reacting part responsible for overt behaviour, defined functionally, p. 80) from S (parameters to R) and from the essential variables (physiological limits: burn, desiccation). The kitten-fire story is the harsh case: essential variables are "immediately affected by the environment only" so that without action the kitten burns — the condition under which adaptation must occur. Immediate effects from R to essential variables would mean the brain could change the organism that must not be burnt into one that benefits from being burnt; Ashby excludes this at the fundamental level (p. 81).

3. **Double feedback is necessary and sufficient for adaptation — and the second feedback is not optional (S. 7/5–7/8, PDF pp. 83–85).** Fig. 7/5/1 (p. 83) shows two loops: the first sensory-motor loop carries "non-affective information about the world" within each reaction; the second loop goes "through the essential variables … it carries information about whether the essential variables are or are not driven outside the normal limits, and it acts on the parameters S. The first feedback plays its part within each reaction; the second determines which reaction shall occur" (p. 83). S. 7/6 traces it operationally: the burned kitten's behaviour changes so S² ≠ S¹, therefore there is an immediate effect essential → S (p. 83). The basic rule (p. 84): `(1) When the essential variables are not all within normal limits … no state of S is to be equilibrial (S must go to some other state). (2) When the essential variables are all within normal limits then every state of S is to be equilibrial (neutral equilibrium).` S. 7/8 proves necessity: without the second channel the system would have to "go to correct values without being given an appropriate input" — "like asking an examiner to accept as natural a candidate who gives the correct answers without being given the questions" (p. 85). S. 7/11 then proves sufficiency via the veto theorem: all equilibria of the whole have essential variables within limits (p. 85).

4. **Step-functions are the way S changes — finite intervals of constancy separated by instantaneous jumps (S. 7/13–7/17, PDF pp. 87–89; typology Fig. 7/13/1).** Four types are distinguished (p. 87): full-function (no constancy interval), part-function (mixed), step-function (Fig. C), null-function (D; i.e. never changes during observation). Ashby notes step-functions "occur abundantly in nature" but are inconspicuous because "things in motion sooner catch the eye" (p. 88), listing eight examples (electric switch, fuse, elastic break, acid-base pH, protein precipitation, quartz adsorption, quantum jumps, all-or-none variables, pp. 88–89). For ultrastability Ashby selects the case "in which the variables in environment and R all vary continuously, while those in S vary discretely (i.e. by finite jumps, occurring at finite intervals)" — not as a claim about brains but because it is "much easier to grasp" (S. 7/13 p. 86) and, per S. 9/4, discretely-jumping variables are "likely sometimes to be of real importance." Variables of Fig. 2/12/1 are typed as full-, part-, step-, step- accordingly (p. 87).

**Why this literature, for this cell.** The field already has four landed readings (DGM recent in `initiative.py`, Argyris misread and Meadows transfer in `initiative_watch.py`, Lawrence critique in `follow_through.py`). The mechanism angle asks for an *operational mechanism this area proposes — something implementable, not just a philosophy*. Ultrastability is that mechanism's canonical statement: a two-feedback, essential-variable-bounded, step-function-mediated, veto-selective architecture that turns "the plant observing and changing itself" from indefinite parameter tuning into a bounded adaptive circuit with stated necessary and sufficient conditions. It connects directly to three predecessors — it formalises Argyris's governing variables as parameters S, Meadows's leverage ladder as which variables are S vs essential, and Lawrence's domination/discipline as systemic veto — while adding what none provides: a mechanistic distinction between *which reaction* (S) and *within a reaction* (R).

## What this plant already does

*The loop observes itself through one feedback, with continuously-typed thresholds and no veto that selects equilibria.*

- **One feedback where Ashby requires two.** `src/mishe_tauftauf/substrate_watch.py:1-120` hashes HEAD/index/tracked files and reports drift against the pin as a freshness fact (`OBSERVED` when pin==HEAD and working tree clean, else `UNKNOWN` with problems). `src/mishe_tauftauf/initiative.py:185` `collect()` scans tape/patches and classifies outcomes `OBSERVED/PARTIAL/UNKNOWN`. `src/mishe_tauftauf/initiative_watch.py:317` `evaluate()` emits findings `aged-wake/continuation-review/producer-stale/patch-gap` and routes them. All three project through `src/mishe_tauftauf/follow_through.py:20` as one composed wall line. This is Ashby's *first* feedback — sensory-motor information flow *within* a reaction (tape → findings → routing). The *second* feedback — essential variables → S (parameters that determine which reaction) — has no separate channel: when an essential variable exceeds limits (evidence digest mismatch, pin drift, service dead) the plant re-emits the same finding at `REMINDER_SECONDS` rather than changing S (the threshold or the vocabulary itself). A repeated `aged-wake:field-review` is therefore routed forever without the step-function jump Ashby makes definitional of adaptation (p. 84, rule 1→2).

- **Governing variables are typed as parameters but not as step-functions.** `src/mishe_tauftauf/initiative_watch.py:52-170` makes `GOVERNING_VARIABLES` explicit as `{value, unit, provenance, role}` with `governing_variables()` and `is_single_loop_trap()` (Argyris), and `MEADOWS_LEVERAGE_POINTS/MEADOWS_RANK/GOVERNING_LEVERAGE/_KIND_LEVERAGE` (Meadows 1999) type leverage depth 12→1. None of these is typed by Ashby's constancy-interval distinction (S. 7/13 Fig. A–D): whether the variable is full-, part-, step- or null-function over the observation window. As a result a threshold that should behave as a step-function (constant while essential variables within limits; jumping only when they are not) reads the same as a continuously-tunable dial. The 4I addition in `follow_through.py:10-55` (`LEARNING_PROCESSES/POWER_FORMS/PROCESS_POWER_MAP/ROUTING_POWER`, Lawrence) types *who* acts when; Ashby types *how* S acts when: constancy until critical-state trigger.

- **Whole-plant equilibrium has no veto predicate.** The three observers each report `state` independently (`OBSERVED`/`UNKNOWN`), but the plant nowhere states Ashby's whole-equilibrium condition (S. 6/9): the whole is at equilibrium iff *each* part is at equilibrium in conditions given by the others, so each part has veto. Today's plant can be `OBSERVED` on substrate (HEAD==pin) yet `UNKNOWN` on initiative (producer stale) and still render the two side-by-side without naming that the stale producer vetoes whole-plant adaptation. Conversely a `SUBSTRATE: UNKNOWN` from pin drift reads as a freshness fact while functioning as a veto on any `wall outcome`'s claim to be current — the veto is exercised in checks (`observer-verdict-agreement.py`, `pin-verdict-agreement.py`) but never typed as "part B vetoes whole."

- **Essential variables are present but not named as such.** The plant does have variables whose exceeding limits should drive S: `EVIDENCE_BINDING_CLAUSE_TIME`, evidence-file digest equality, `HEAD==pin`, service MainPID liveness, `MAX_BYTES` overflow. They are checked as `problems` arrays and `state=UNKNOWN` flags, not as Ashby's essential-variable dial with two limit-marks (Fig. 7/3/1 p. 81) whose collective within-limits condition is exactly S's equilibrium condition (p. 84: S in neutral equilibrium iff essential variables all within limits).

## What is new here

**Separate the two feedbacks, name the essential variables as one dial, type S-variables as step-functions, and expose the veto that selects equilibria — so the plant can distinguish "another reminder of the same finding" from "change S because the essential variable left limits."**

Three ideas transfer directly, each with a line number where it would land, each bounded by what the source concedes.

1. **Essential variables are the adaptational load-bearers (S. 7/3 Fig. 7/3/1).** Collect the plant's non-negotiables — evidence-digest binding (`artifacts/` SHA256), HEAD==pin exact-SHA, service MainPID > 0 / renderer liveness, site ignored / not carried — as `ULTRASTABLE_ESSENTIALS: Final[dict[str, dict]]` beside `VERSION/SOURCE` in `substrate_watch.py`. Each entry carries `{meaning, limit, provenance}` and a module docstring cites Ashby 1960 S. 7/3 p. 81 (environment → essential only) and p. 84 rule (1)/(2). The existing `_checkout()` problems and `_runtime()` problems remain the reporters; the new constant only names *which* problems are essential-variable exceedances whose within-limits condition is S's neutral equilibrium.

2. **Step-functions type how a governing variable changes (S. 7/13 Fig. 7/13/1).** Add `STEP_VARS: Final[set[str]]` (or `VARIABLE_FUNCTION_TYPE: Final[dict[str,str]]` mapping to `full/part/step/null`) classifying the plant's governance variables by their constancy interval: `AGED_SECONDS/CONTINUATION_SECONDS/PRODUCER_MAX_AGE/ESCALATE_SECONDS/REMINDER_SECONDS` are `step` (finite constancy, instantaneous jump at critical state defined by essential-variable violation); `WINDOW_SECONDS/MAX_PATCHES/MAX_TAPE_BYTES` are `step` at the window/rollover, `MAX_BYTES` budgets `step` at overflow. The helper `is_step_function(var: str) -> bool | None` is pure/deterministic. This preserves Meadows's leverage typing and Argyris's governing-variable explicitness while adding Ashby's mechanism: within limits S is constant (no adaptation); outside limits S jumps (adaptation by trial).

3. **Veto is the selector (S. 6/9–6/10).** Add a pure predicate `whole_equilibrium_requires_all_parts(part_states: dict[str,str]) -> bool` and `veto_holders(part_states: dict[str,str]) -> list[str]` — deterministic, no I/O, returns `True` (or list) when every named part reports `OBSERVED`/`KNOWN` in its own conditions, else names which part vetoes the whole. Docstring cites Ashby p. 78–79: "every part has a power of veto" and "the whole's being at equilibrium implies each part must be at equilibrium … conversely … whole will have equilibrium." Today `follow_through.py:20` composes lines; the new predicate types the *selection* behind those lines: which equilibria remain possible after each veto, without adding a new `state` word.

**Limits the proposal keeps from the source.** Ashby's 1960 text offers no empirical test of a brain; it offers the homeostat (S. 8–9) as existence proof and, per S. 7/12 p. 86, leaves "a great number of supplementary questions … unanswered" — the step-function discreteness is introduced explicitly for easier apprehension (p. 86) before S. 9/4 argues it may be physiologically real, and the continuous-vs-discrete question is left as "should one of them occur, the step-function will [jump]" (p. 89). The transfer is therefore a typed vocabulary/detector, not a claim that discretising a threshold causes any particular `aged-wake` to clear this week, nor a change in what the plant automatically adapts to. Continuous-approximation and mixed-form variables remain admissible; the type records the interval, not the cause. Conant-Ashby (1970) "good regulator" and requisite-variety (Ashby 1956) are not invoked here.
