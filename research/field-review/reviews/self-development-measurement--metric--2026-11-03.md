- Verbatims kept in this review (all from PMC HTML paragraphs P6–P24 on 2026-11-03 extraction):
  - "Statistical significance is the least interesting thing about the results. You should describe the results in terms of measures of magnitude — not just, does a treatment affect people, but how much does it affect them." — Gene V. Glass (epigraph p.279).
  - "The primary product of a research inquiry is one or more measures of effect size, not P values." — Jacob Cohen (epigraph p.279).
  - Definition p.279: "In medical education research studies that compare different educational interventions, effect size is the magnitude of the difference between groups. The absolute effect size is the difference between the average, or mean, outcomes in two different intervention groups."
  - Standardized form p.280: "For two independent groups, effect size can be measured by the standardized difference between two means, or mean (group 1) – mean (group 2) / standard deviation. The denominator standardizes the difference by transforming the absolute difference into standard deviation units. Cohen's term d is an example of this type of effect size index."
  - Cohen's thresholds p.280: "Cohen classified effect sizes as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), and large (d ≥ 0.8). According to Cohen, 'a medium effect of .5 is visible to the naked eye of a careful observer. A small effect of .2 is noticeably smaller than medium but not so small as to be trivial. A large effect of .8 is the same distance above the medium as small is below it.'"
  - Percentile/overlap reading p.280: "For an effect size of 0, the mean of group 2 is at the 50th percentile of group 1, and the distributions overlap completely (100%)—that is, there is no difference. For an effect size of 0.8, the mean of group 2 is at the 79th percentile of group 1; thus, someone from group 2 with an average score (i.e., mean) would have a higher score than 79% of the people from group 1. The distributions overlap by only 53% or a non-overlap of 47% in this situation (table 2)."
  - P-value vs effect size p.280: "While a P value can inform the reader whether an effect exists, the P value will not reveal the size of the effect. In reporting and interpreting studies, both the substantive significance (effect size) and statistical significance (P value) are essential results to be reported." And p.280: "With a sufficiently large sample, a statistical test will almost always demonstrate a significant difference, unless there is no effect whatsoever, that is, when the effect size is exactly zero; yet very small differences, even if significant, are often meaningless. … Unlike significance tests, effect size is independent of sample size. Statistical significance, on the other hand, depends upon both sample size and effect size. For this reason, P values are considered to be confounded because of their dependence on sample size."
  - Physicians' Health Study example p.280: aspirin vs MI with n>22 000, P<.00001 but "risk difference of 0.77% with r²=.001—an extremely small effect size" — small-but-significant caution.
  - Power paragraph p.281: "Before starting your study, calculate the power of your study with an estimated effect size; if power is too low, you may need more subjects … effect size can be estimated by pilot study results, similar work published by others, or the minimum difference that would be considered important by educators/experts."

## What is new here

- Sullivan & Feinn's transferable metric for this plant is the *standardized effect size* — not a significance test. Where the existing `q` divides by mean |Δ| (mean absolute swing), `d`/`Δ` divides by SD: `d = (trial − baseline) / SD_placebo` (or Glass's Δ with one group's SD when variances differ). The result reads as "this difference is N standard deviations of ordinary window-to-window variation", with Cohen's conventional anchors small 0.2 / medium 0.5 visible / large 0.8 and a distribution gloss (0→50th percentile 100% overlap; 0.8→79th percentile 47% non-overlap) that absolute counts cannot give.
- The paper's central distinction is new for the report: magnitude (effect size) is *independent of sample size* (here: independent of entries-per-window and of K), while statistical significance / rank is *confounded* by it. With 8 894 entries in a busy window vs 1 087 in a quiet one, a count delta of fixed absolute size will appear more "significant" by rank/p alone even when its SD-standardized magnitude is tiny — exactly Sullivan's aspirin caution with n>22 000, P<.00001 yet r²=.001. The trial report currently invites reading rank/p-like rarity without a magnitude anchor; reporting both separates the two questions as the paper directs: "both the substantive significance (effect size) and statistical significance (P value) are essential".
- Planning lesson: effect size belongs *before* the study too — estimate by pilot (prior placebo windows), similar work, or minimum important difference — to size the trial and avoid Type II β error. For this plant that means the report can state the minimum detectable d given current placebo SD and K, not just the post-hoc delta.
- Why distinct from MASE: MASE's denominator is mean |Δ| between consecutive placebos (robust to level, sensitive to adjacent jitter); d/Δ's denominator is SD across placebos (captures overall spread, enables percentile/overlap reading). When placebo counts are autocorrelated or heteroskedastic, the two disagree — and that disagreement is informative. Sullivan also notes absolute effect size remains useful when the variable has intrinsic meaning (e.g., hours of sleep; here: raw wake counts) — so the absolute delta stays, d/Δ is an added column, not a replacement.
- Limits in the source's own terms and ours: (a) Cohen's 0.2/0.5/0.8 are conventions "not taking into account other variables such as the accuracy of the assessment instrument" — here instrument = feed counting via prefixes/lifecycle decode, so thresholds are rough glosses, not verdicts. (b) Standardizing assumes the denominator estimates population SD; with K≤10 and floor-gated kept windows (often n≈6–8 after outages), SD is noisy and a window with zero spread makes d undefined — must read UNKNOWN, mirroring MASE zero-denominator handling. (c) Feed counts per 10 h are not normal i.i.d.; the percentile/overlap mapping (79th, 53% overlap at d=0.8) assumes approximate normality — report it as a conventional gloss, not a distributional fact. (d) Glass's Δ vs pooled d matters when placebo vs trial variance differs; with one trial window, pooled SD is not defined — Glass's form `(trial−µ_placebo)/σ_placebo` (or `(trial−baseline)/σ_placebo` matching MASE numerator) is the honest variant here. (e) Does not replace rank, q, CUSUM or DID audit — they answer rarity, scale-free swing, sequential drift, and conditional identification; d/Δ answers magnitude in SD units.

- Add pure helpers beside `placebo_scale()` / `_maturation_trend()`:

  `standardized_effect_for_counts(placebo_series: list[int|float], trial: float, baseline: float, *, which: str = "glass") -> dict` — computes Glass's Δ (default) as `(trial − baseline) / sd_placebo` where `sd_placebo` is the sample SD of the kept placebo series (same windows 2..11 floor-gated, trial excluded, matching `placebo_scale` kept). Return `{"d": float|None, "status": "ok"|"UNKNOWN", "reason": str, "sd": float|None, "mu": float|None, "n": int, "gloss": str}`. UNKNOWN when `n<3` or `sd==0` or non-finite (mirroring existing UNKNOWN for MASE denominator), never divide by zero. When `which="cohen"` and a second trial series were available, pooled form could be noted but not used here (single trial point). `gloss` maps |d| to Sullivan/Cohen anchors: `<0.2 trivial`, `0.2–0.5 small`, `0.5–0.8 medium`, `≥0.8 large`, plus percentile/overlap note for the 0 and 0.8 examples as in the paper ("d=0.8 → mean at 79th percentile, 53% overlap / 47% non-overlap") as conventional gloss.

  Thin wrapper `effect_size_audit_for_counts(placebo: dict, sample: dict, baseline: dict) -> dict[str, dict]` that pulls per-count kept series from `placebo["_window_counts"]` (or recomputed series when absent) and returns per-count dicts via the helper, so `report()` can call it with the dicts it already holds.

- Extend `report()` to print, below the existing scaled-difference (q) lines and beside Huang/Campbell/Deaton blocks, one additive line per count:

  `Effect size (Sullivan d/Glass Δ): wakes d=… (sd=… mu=… n=…; small|medium|large; 79th/53% gloss at 0.8) — magnitude independent of n; report alongside rank/p, not instead — UNKNOWN if sd==0 or n<3`

- Tests (pure, no filesystem) to keep:

  - Zero-spread: placebo series `[5,5,5,5]` → status UNKNOWN, d None, no division.
  - n<3: series `[10,12]` → UNKNOWN.
  - No-shift: placebo `~N(100,15)` and trial==baseline at mu → |d|<0.2 trivial, not large.
  - Large shift: mu=100 sd=15 trial=112 baseline=100 → d≈0.8 → gloss large, 79th percentile note present.
  - Trial exclusion: helper built from `placebo_scale` kept windows excludes the trial window from mu/sd estimation.
  - Coexistence: with synthetic placebo where mean|Δ| small but SD large, q and d diverge — demonstrating why both magnitude columns are needed (MASE vs SD-standardized).

- Limits to print with the block: (a) Cohen 0.2/0.5/0.8 are conventional, instrument-dependent glosses, not decision thresholds. (b) n≈6–10 makes SD noisy; percentile/overlap assumes approximate normality — reported as conventional gloss. (c) With one trial point, pooled SD not defined — Glass's form is the honest variant. (d) Needs in-control placebo reference; outbreak/outage windows already floor-excluded but remaining regime shift still contaminates SD — mark UNKNOWN or "reference not in control" when appropriate rather than printing a magnitude.

Prediction: on current `main` with ordinary placebo windows (n≈6–10, sd>0) most counts report |d|<0.5 (small) because one trial window is one of several ordinary windows; a `LIMITED`-capacity episode that adds a persistent offset before the trial window will push that count toward medium/large d while rank alone may remain inside placebo range, surfacing the Sullivan distinction.
