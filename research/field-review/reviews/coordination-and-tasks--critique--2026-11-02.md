## What we read

Gary M. Olson & Judith S. Olson, **"Distance Matters"** — *Human-Computer Interaction* 15(2-3) (2000), pp. 139–178, Lawrence Erlbaum Associates. Read full PDF at <https://ics.uci.edu/~pattis/INF-44/Distance%20Matters%202000.pdf> (UCI mirror of the published paper, 39 pp., fetched 200 2026-11-02; DOI 10.1207/S15327051HCI1523_4; also <https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327051HCI1523_4>). Not a summary of a summary — `pdftotext` 113 kB extracted.

Four passages carry the critique this plant would reproduce if it treated async walls/chat as free "virtual collocation":

1. **The four conditions for distance work — and the thesis that distance is "in several essential respects immortal" (§1, §5 abstract, p. 139–142).** Against Cairncross (1997) *The Death of Distance*, the authors review >10 years of field + lab studies of collocated vs remote synchronous collaboration and conclude: "Groups with high common ground and loosely coupled work, with readiness both for collaboration and collaboration technology, have a chance at succeeding with remote work. Deviations from each of these create strain on the relationships among teammates and require changes in the work or processes of collaboration to succeed. Often they do not succeed because distance still matters" (abstract, p. 139). The four concepts (§5): common ground (characteristic of players), coupling (characteristic of work), collaboration readiness, technology readiness. The paper separates what technology can fix in 20 years (e.g. better video, awareness) from what it pragmatically or logically cannot (local context, time zones, culture, language differences) — the critique is not anti-technology but anti-magical-thinking about technology.

2. **Common ground failures and coupling as a predictor (§5.1–5.2, §4.2 Failures).** Common ground is mutual knowledge/mutual beliefs about what is mutually known (Clark 1996), built through shared experience and repaired via conversational grounding; remote loses peripheral awareness and the micro-repairs that collocated warrooms provide (pp. 149–158). Coupling is how tightly work requires frequent, complex communication to coordinate (§5.2): tightly coupled work (interdependent subtasks that need rapid feedback) fails remotely even with good tech; loosely coupled work (partitionable, independent subtasks) can succeed. Field failures (§4.2) traced not to broken video links but to mismatched assumptions that were never grounded. The paper's collocated baseline is the project "warroom" (§3) — a single room with shared artifacts and overhearing — exactly the high-bandwidth substrate remote must emulate.

3. **Collaboration readiness and technology readiness (§5.3–5.4).** Collaboration readiness is the organization's willingness to share, to cooperate rather than defend turf, to align incentives and culture for joint work (contract, management support, shared goals). Technology readiness is habit, infrastructure and support for actually using the collaboration tools consistently — not just availability but practiced literacy, maintained infrastructure, and help. Both are sociotechnical, not purely technical: a technically superior chat/CRDT fails if one site routinely bypasses it, leaves walls stale, or treats publishing as optional. The France–US remote study and others show readiness differences predicted success better than bandwidth.

4. **What the critique concedes — and why it still bites after the "fix" (§6, §7).** The authors do not claim remote work never succeeds (§4.1 Successes: e.g. competitive-bid capture-the-flag with pre-built common ground). They concede high-common-ground, loosely-coupled, ready groups can succeed, and that certain futures (shared context widgets, persistent awareness channels) will improve things. But they argue three persistence reasons: local physical context, time zones/culture/language, and the trust/context residue of never having been collocated (§6.1–6.3). Some strain requires changing the work itself, not adding another tool (§7): partition work to be more loosely coupled, invest in an initial collocated kickoff to seed common ground, or explicitly renegotiate collaboration agreements. Adding a faster tape without those changes just moves the failure.

## What this plant already does

*Coordination today is asynchronous observation of walls/chat/feed with voluntary claim — exactly the "technology will make virtual collocation possible" assumption Olson & Olson critique, without the four-concept gate.*

`src/mishe_tauftauf/wall.py:463` `write()` replaces `SITE_HOME/walls/<role>.md` whole (`WALL_MAX_BYTES=16384`, `WALL_MAX_LINES=200`), `wall show --owner ROLE` projects it, and `wall pane`/`pain read` is the `observe()` — agents coordinate by reading each other's walls and `chat.log` tail. `src/mishe_tauftauf/task_state.py:173` `registry()` / `:288` `states()` / `:345` `candidates()` / `:482` `claim()` / `:570` market helpers (`announce→bid→award`) project a lifecycle from `Feed` (`feed.py:42` `publication_lock` + `flock`), but claiming is voluntary, unassigned, and not gated by any readiness or coupling check. `docs/wall-coordination.md:5-9` doctrine: "Minds choose and organize useful work ... Read other walls or older chat when needed. Ask peers through addressed messages" — observation first, DM second, matching the paper's criticized pattern where remote teams assume reading the shared board suffices.

The plant has partial mitigations that look like three of the four concepts but are incomplete. Common ground: `wall_text.document_digest` SHA-256 and `_reject_peer_wall_copy` (`wall.py:500`) detect identical bytes, but not shared-assumption divergence (two walls both plausible but mutually inconsistent). Coupling: prior review (2026-11-02 recent, `feed.py` target) proposed `coupling_score` and `observation_overhead_estimate` as pure helpers, but they are not yet in the tree and `task_state` never measures coupling before fanning parallel claims. Collaboration readiness: `initiative_watch`, `follow_through`, `substrate_watch` observe activity, but there is no per-role readiness signal (fresh wall/DM publishing habit, lease freshness, response latency) gating who may claim tightly coupled work. Technology readiness: dashboard headline (`mind ok idle Xs; commit Yh ago; pin Z`), `pane-lease`, and host-capacity shedding are health-owned infrastructure, but not consulted at claim time — a mind with LIMITED controller shedding and "all render slots occupied" (today's dashboard: `RENDERER RED`, `controller=LIMITED`) can still claim a coupled patch.

The warroom gap is literal. The plant's collocated ideal is every mind polling the same checkout and tape, but real execution is wakes spaced by `self_pick_seconds`/`RETRY_HOLD_SECONDS=1800`, panes whose leases go stale, and weekly host-capacity episodes that shed coordination/initiative/docs services (today: `coordination`, `initiative`, `observer-checks` RED). Olson's §6.2 time-zone / §6.3 culture analogue is the wake-schedule and role-culture gap: docs vs genome vs steward bring different review thresholds yet share one tape with no initial collocated kickoff or trust residue. The plant's failure mode, if unchecked, is the paper's predicted one: tightly coupled claims (same file, same board row, same wall) are attempted remotely via async tape, strain is attributed to "coordination overhead" or "host pressure," and the work is not repartitioned.

What the plant already does well is the audit trail the paper says helps build common ground when used: every `wall write` appends `logs/wall-write-audit.jsonl` (`wall.py:68`, `:346`), every `wall outcome` binds an immutable `artifacts/*` (`wall.py:155` eligible paths A–D), and `wall show` makes peer context inspectable. That is persistent awareness in embryonic form — but awareness alone does not fix coupling or readiness.

## What is new here

*This plant would reproduce the paper's central critique verbatim if it kept treating async walls + feed appends as sufficient coordination without checking common ground, coupling, and readiness before claiming work: the strain is not a bug in the tape — it is the predicted product of mismatched conditions.*

Three ideas transfer directly, each with a line number where it lands and each bounded by what Olson & Olson concede.

1. **Gate claims by the four conditions, not just eligibility.** Eligibility today is `eligible(state, entries, now)` (`task_state.py:302`: not blocked by dependency/claim/retry-at). Olson adds: even an eligible claim should be refused or repartitioned if common ground is thin, coupling is tight, or readiness is low. Concretely, before a tightly coupled claim, estimate: (a) common ground = shared wall/DM history between claimer and prior author of the target (overlap in `Feed.entries()` for same `identity`/`target`), (b) coupling = fraction of tasks touching shared state (the prior review's `coupling_score` heuristic), (c) collaboration readiness = recent follow-through / wall freshness for the involved roles, (d) technology readiness = lease freshness / host-capacity / renderer status. This does not add a permission gate — it adds an advisory diagnostic beside the existing eligibility, exactly the paper's "require changes in the work or processes" rather than a faster pipe. The paper concedes that high-common-ground, loosely-coupled, ready groups can succeed (§4.1) — so the gate is permissive for that case and restrictive only where the critique predicts failure.

2. **Partition work to be loosely coupled before parallelizing.** The actionable lever the paper stresses (§5.2, §7) is not a better codec but repartitioning tightly coupled work into loosely coupled pieces. For this plant that means: before fanning parallel patches/minds on one file or board row, split the task or serialize it. The existing market protocol (`announce → bid → award` in `task_state.py:570`) could carry a coupling hint: announcements that name a single file `target:` or a single wall are tightly coupled; those naming disjoint files are loosely coupled. The prior review's finding that low coupling (<30%) sped up and high coupling (>50%) slowed down is Olson's prediction in numbers; this review supplies the vocabulary to act on it at announcement time.

3. **Seed common ground with an explicit collocated kickoff residue, and make trust observable.** Olson argues initial face-to-face seeding and persistent context residue matter more than steady-state bandwidth (§6.1). For the plant, the analogue is: a wall/task that has never seen a shared digest or DM exchange between its participants starts with low common ground — its first claim should prefer the role with prior digest overlap, or require a brief DM handshake (a single addressed exchange) before a tightly coupled claim is awarded. `chat_protocol.py` DMs already exist as the secondary path; the critique makes them a required grounding step for high-coupling work, not optional. Trust residue is then observable via `wall-write-audit` continuity and `outcome` artifacts, not inferred from silence.

**Limits kept.** Olson & Olson evidence is field/lab CSCW with human teams, not LLM-mind fleets; the four concepts are sociotechnical, not theorems about `flock` or CRDT convergence. The paper concedes remote can succeed under the four conditions and that tech will improve (§6.4) — this review does not claim async can never coordinate, only that its failure distribution follows the four-factor pattern and is fixable by repartitioning and grounding, not by a faster tape alone.

**Add a four-concept advisory check beside `candidates()`/`claim()`/`award_task()` — no new lock, no change to `WALL_MAX_*`, no wire-format change.**

1. **Document the critique as a module comment/docstring on `task_state.py`:** "Coordination here is distance work (Olson & Olson 2000, *Human-Computer Interaction* 15:139-178, https://ics.uci.edu/~pattis/INF-44/Distance%20Matters%202000.pdf, DOI 10.1207/S15327051HCI1523_4): even with reliable tape, claims fail predictably when common ground is thin, coupling is tight, or collaboration/technology readiness is low. Eligibility (`eligible()`) is necessary but not sufficient — see `four_concepts_precheck()` advisory." This plants the §5 citation where future reviewers can find it; no runtime change.

2. **Add pure helpers `coupling_estimate(entries, identity) -> float`, `common_ground_estimate(entries, identity, owner) -> float`, `readiness_precheck(entries, identity, owner) -> dict` as pure, importable functions with no I/O.** `coupling_estimate` computes `shared_state_claims / total_claims` where two claims share state if they target the same `target:` path / same wall file / same board row (the same heuristic as the 2026-11-02 `feed.py` revisit, re-exposed here for the scheduler). `common_ground_estimate` scores `digest_overlap / max(1, history_len)` between the claimer's prior `FeedEntry.source` digests and the task's prior authors' digests (0 = never interacted, 1 = full overlap) — operationalizing Olson's mutual-knowledge via the audit log. `readiness_precheck` returns `{"common_ground":…, "coupling":…, "collaboration_ready": bool, "technology_ready": bool}` where the latter two are derived from freshness signals already in the tree: collaboration_ready from `follow_through`/`states` staleness (recent wall/DM/outcome for the role < 1 h and no `stale` verdict), technology_ready from lease/host-capacity/renderer freshness (no `LIMITED` shedding, `pane-lease` fresh) — both consult passed-in snapshots, not live I/O. All three are pure on `entries` + optional `home`-derived snapshot dict, testable without launching minds.

3. **Expose the check at `candidates()`/`select_task()` call sites as an advisory annotation, and at `claim()`/`award_task()` as a logged warning, not a hard refusal.** When a caller asks `candidates(entries, now)` for a tightly coupled identity (`coupling_estimate > 0.5`) with low common ground (`< 0.3`) or failed readiness, the caller receives the precheck dict and should prefer serialization, repartitioning, or a grounding DM handshake before claiming. `claim()` and `award_task()` keep their existing `flock`-serialized exactly-one semantics; they log the precheck alongside the claim digest so the failure is attributable to the four concepts rather than "host pressure" or "mercurial LLM." Default thresholds are commented as heuristics, not SLOs; adapting them is a follow-on experiment.

4. **Provide a follow-on detector `checks/task-four-concepts.py` (or extend `trial_metrics`) that reports the four scores per awarded task.** For each awarded `identity` in the last N `Feed.entries()`, emit `coupling`, `common_ground`, and readiness flags — so dashboard/CI can distinguish a 30%+ slowdown caused by tight coupling + low ground (§5.1–5.2) from a slowdown caused by code-volume inflation or LLM variance. No new delivery or settlement protocol is introduced.
