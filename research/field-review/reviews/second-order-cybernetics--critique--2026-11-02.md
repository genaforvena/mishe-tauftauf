The critique is of second-order cybernetics' observer-centric paradigm. Von Foerster (1974) distinguished "the cybernetics of observed systems" (1oC) and "the cybernetics of observing systems" (2oC) (p.02). Ashby argues "the abstract conceptualization of a pure observer has no purpose except observing, which is like a person who is meditating so deeply with open unfocussed gazing eyes that they neither react … nor make memories" (p.02), and "because there are an infinite number of possible reasons why an observer might observe a system, the term 'observer' is completely ambiguous with respect to purpose, which renders it deficient in clarity and utility" (p.02). This, he argues, made 1974 "the year of the schism … into … 'the cybernetics of regulated systems' and 'the philosophy of cybernetics'" (p.03), attenuated "the scientific study of the cybernetics of reflexive systems" (p.04), and by von Foerster's own 1990 interview claim — "it would not create anything new, because by ascending into 'second-order,' … one has stepped into the circle that closes upon itself" (p.04) and Glanville's collapse claim "a third-order system cannot exist because it collapses into being equivalent to a first-order system" (p.04) — blocks a meaningful third-order.

The alternative is a model-centric paradigm from Wiener's "scientific study of control and communication" (1948) via the Conant-Ashby good regulator theorem: "every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system" (p.05, [9]). A first-order regulator R1 consists of purpose P1, model M1, observer O1, intelligence I1 and channel C1; "because a fundamental characteristic of a model is that it requires observations as inputs, the model not only brings into existence the need for an observer … but that observer is … well-defined by the needs and purpose of [the] second model" and "the model is determined by the purpose of the regulator. So … O1 is to M1 and I1 as a keyboard is to a program and a computer" (pp.05–06). R2 adds a self-model M2/O2/C2/I2/P2 for reflexivity (FOMC six-week cadence, two-armed robot examples, pp.06–07); R3 adds a model of acceptable (ethical) behaviour M3 with O3 monitoring intended actions of I1 before execution and I3 vetoing via C3 as conscience (pp.07–10, Ross Ashby 1941 cortex break Table 2 p.09, Soon et al. 2008 10s delay p.09). Table 3 (p.11) summarises 1oC/simple/system, 2oC/reflexive/regulator-itself, 3oC/ethical/acceptable-behaviour.

Concession/limit the critique itself makes: the paper states "This paper's criticism … is not intended to suggest that it has not been useful. Even if it has weaknesses, it made an important distinction that helped cybernetics develop" (p.04), that philosophical interpretations added "incredible richness" (p.04), and that the science/philosophy distinction has "significant complementary overlap" (p.03). The schism diagnosis is historical, not a claim that SOC is refuted; the model-centric construction is presented as more useful for practical regulation, not as proof the observer-centric reading is false. The third-order examples (cortisol/dopamine, empathy, Asimov's First Law) remain speculative and normative.

Limits of this reading: the source is a conceptual/historical argument, not a detector spec; R3's visceral-signal mapping and under-18 media prescription are not used. I use only the abstract-observer ambiguity diagnosis (pp.02–04) and the M-defines-O / R1/R2/R3 triad (pp.05–11).

## What is new here

The new piece from this critique is the name for a failure the plant would reproduce if it adopted SOC blindly: treating `observer` as a role that can be named once and reused across detectors. Ashby's test is purpose → model → observer, not observer → purpose. An observer without a stated purpose and model is "completely ambiguous" (p.02) and cannot be distinguished from any other activity that requires observations; calling the verdict comparer, the scheduler-coverage scanner, and the wall-review hash checker each an "observer" hides that they exist for different purposes and must collect different information.

For this plant, the transfer is threefold and none of it is in the tree:

1. **M defines O.** The observer in `observer_verdict_watch.py` is not an abstract observer but O1 defined by M1 = `VERDICT_FIELDS` plus the interval/cutoff purpose "route a disagreement at once, an UNKNOWN only when persistent" (ll.09–12). Naming it as model-defined makes the contract testable: change `VERDICT_FIELDS` or the cutoff and the observer's required inputs change; change the purpose (e.g., ethical veto) and a different observer is needed. This is the keyboard/program/computer analogy (p.06) applied to the watcher's constants.

2. **R1 ≠ R2 ≠ R3.** Ashby's triad reframes the plant's already-named R2 reflexivity (the scheduler-coverage detector observing whether checks are observed) as distinct from an R3 conscience. The plant's verdict agreement check is R1 (simple): it models the regulated system (the verdict). The scheduler-coverage check is R2 (reflexive): it models the regulator itself (which checks are scheduled). Neither is R3 (ethical): von Foerster's circle and Glanville's collapse claim "would not create anything new" (p.04) are, in Ashby's terms, demonstrably false for a veto-before-execution conscience (M3/O3/C3, pp.07–10) — the permission/conscience path that does not collapse into R1. The critique thus warns against reading any "observing system" as already ethical.

3. **1974 schism as design caution.** The paper's historical claim that observer-centric language encouraged "profound intellectual inclusiveness … not without drawbacks" and "many alternative definitions" (Table 1 p.03–04) predicts the exact failure of detectors whose `observer` name is shared without a purpose table: definitions multiply, verification does not. The plant's existing `SELF_NAME` blind-spot paragraph (check-scheduler-coverage.py ll.121–122, now complemented by Zönnchen 2025) already fixes one instance; Ashby extends the fix to all verdict/watcher headers.

Dissent within the literature is part of the reading: SOC defenders (von Foerster, Glanville) treat the circle as closure and deny a meaningful third-order; Ashby treats it as completeness failure ("The Ethics Problem", p.11) and argues 2oC is "ethically agnostic … cannot credibly claim to be … the cybernetics of ethical systems" (p.11). The review does not adjudicate this dispute; it applies only the diagnostic that abstract observers are ambiguous and that model-defines-observer is the check.
