## What we read

Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov — **"Robust De-anonymization of Large Datasets (How to Break Anonymity of the Netflix Prize Dataset)"**, arXiv:cs/0610105v2 [cs.CR] 22 Nov 2007, 6 pp. — read full author PDF at:

- <https://arxiv.org/abs/cs/0610105> (canonical arXiv record, v2 2007-11-22)
- <https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0610105.pdf> (fetched 200 2026-11-02, 640 KB, PDF 1.4 6 pages, 1000 text lines via pdftotext)

We also opened the paper's §2 citations for context but did not separately read them as primary sources for this cell: Sweeney 2002 k-anonymity [24][23][8], Machanavajjhala et al. 2007 ℓ-diversity [17], Li et al. 2007 t-closeness [18], and Aggarwal 2005 high-dimensional failure [2] cited §2 p.2. The review's claims are grounded in Narayanan's own text.

Four passages carry the critique this plant would reproduce if it treated "removed identifiers" or "k-anonymous histograms" as a privacy guarantee:

1. **No a priori quasi-identifier — adversary knowledge is any sparse attribute subset (§§3–4, pp.1–4).** Unlike k-anonymity which "must determine in advance which … are available to the adversary (these are called 'quasi-identifiers')" (p.2 [24][23][8]), Narayanan's model "does not assume a priori that the adversary's knowledge is limited to a fixed set of 'quasi-identifier' attributes" (Abstract + §2 p.2 + §3 pp.3–4). Aux is "an arbitrary probabilistic function Aux: X^M -> X^M" restricted to "a subset of the (possibly imprecise …) values of r's attributes" (p.4 adversary model). If the adversary happens to know some attribute value, it "becomes part of his auxiliary information and thus an 'identifier' (but only for this individual's record)" (p.4). Privacy breach is defined without assuming which columns are identifying.

2. **Very little auxiliary knowledge suffices on sparse, high-dimensional micro-data (§§3–4, Thm 1–3, pp.3–8).** Micro-data are "high dimensionality and sparsity … each pair of random records are located far apart" and "related to the 'fat tail' phenomenon: individual … records tend to include statistically rare attributes" (§1 p.1, §3 p.3 power law [6][4]). Thm 1 (p.7): m >= log N / -log(1-delta) random attribute values with Sim >= 1-epsilon suffice for (1-epsilon-delta, 1-epsilon)-deanonymization. For (1-epsilon-delta, epsilon)-sparse D, Thm 2 gives (1, 1-epsilon)-deanonymization — the matching set is "exactly one record: the true target record r" (p.8). Thm 3: m = log(N/(k-1))/log(1/(1-delta)) gives (1,1/k) and (1, log k) entropic de-anonymization — a k-sized lineup. Case study: "very little background knowledge is needed (as few as 5-10 attributes …) … 2-8 ratings per user … which need not be precise" (Abstract + §5 pp.9–10).

3. **Robustness to perturbation and to publication of a sample (§§3–4, pp.3–5, 8–9).** "There is no difference between the perturbation of the published records and the imprecision of the adversary's knowledge" (p.4); Algorithm 1A scores by "least similar attribute" Score = min_i Sim(aux_i, r'_i) (p.6), Algorithm 1B by weighted sum with wt(i)=1/log|supp(i)| favouring rare attributes + eccentricity gate (p.6). "If the data publisher may only release a (possibly non-uniform) sample … our de-anonymization algorithm [works] even if only a subset … has been published" (p.3 + Thm 4–5 pp.8–9). Sampling "cannot be large, otherwise its utility will be lost" — tailoring a sample to hide nearest neighbours "would completely destroy utility … for collaborative filtering" (p.4, §4.3 p.9). Netflix released ~1/8 of its records and it remained de-anonymizable.

4. **k-anonymization does not guarantee privacy and fails on high-dimensional data (§2 p.2).** "It is well-known that k-anonymity does not guarantee privacy, because the values of sensitive attributes … may not be sufficiently diverse [17,18] or because the adversary has access to background knowledge [17]" and "k-anonymization completely fails on high-dimensional datasets [2], such as the Netflix Prize dataset and most real-world datasets of individual recommendations and purchases" (p.2, citing Aggarwal [2]). Knowing the anonymization algorithm itself "may be sufficient to break privacy [27]" (p.2).

What the paper concedes and what the transfer must keep (pp.2, 6, 9–10): statistical databases [26][1][3][7][5] are out of scope — the attack targets micro-data where "actual records of individuals" remain (p.2); generalization/suppression/MaxSup and distance-vector minimal generalisation [Def. 3.2–3.3, Def. 4.3] are not implemented here — this review leaves them unimplemented and does not propose implementing them; the scoring function's rarity weighting and eccentricity are heuristics that require tuning to a specific dataset's Sim.

## What this plant already does

## What is new here

**The plant's current posture — closed-world CI + descriptive k-anonymity + broadcast publication — would reproduce the exact failure Narayanan proves if it treated the metric as a guarantee: sparse body micro-data is re-identifiable with a handful of outside attributes, without assuming which columns are quasi-identifiers, robustly to perturbation and to sample publication.**

- **Quasi-identifier assumption is the vulnerability, not the fix (Narayanan §2–3 vs plant's QI_DEFAULT_KEYS).** The plant's `QI_DEFAULT_KEYS=("light_class",)` is a single, coarse key chosen for display. An adversary who knows `ble_scan` peers, `wifi_link_rssi_dbm` at a known time, or `inclination_to_gravity_deg` is not limited to that key — under Narayanan's model each of those is an "identifier but only for this individual's record" (p.4). A tail where each `light_class` bin has k>=2 can still be de-anonymized via 5–10 auxiliary attributes drawn from other columns; k-anonymity on one QI does not prevent linkage on another. Aggarwal [2] failure on high-dimensional data is the mechanism.

- **Few attributes suffice because body traces are sparse and high-dimensional (Narayanan §§1,3 Thm 1–3 vs DENSE_TAIL_BYTES/DENSE_WINDOW_ROWS).** The dense queue is canonical micro-data: ~2500 sparse rows over `(light, light_class, acceleration, inclination, wifi_*, ble_scan, battery)` with heavy-tailed rare values (e.g., a specific `ble_scan` peer, a rare `very-bright` spike, an exact `wifi_link_rssi_dbm` sequence). Thm 2 says a sparse dataset needs only `log N / log(1/(1-delta))` random attributes for unique re-identification; for N=2500 and delta small this is 5–8. An outside observer who knows "you were in dark at time X, moved at X+5s, near SSID Y" has that many. Publishing raw dense rows or exact histograms `T[QI]` preserves the sparsity the attack exploits; perturbation of a few lux or date fields does not defeat Score = min Sim (Algorithm 1A) or weighted rare-attribute Score (Algorithm 1B).

- **Publishing a sample does not mitigate (Narayanan Thm 4–5 vs wall-outcome broadcast).** Quoting two body lines in a wall outcome is publishing a sample of the micro-data. Thm 4: a sample `D^` can be (1-epsilon-delta, 1-epsilon)-deanonymized w.r.t. aux exactly as the full D; Thm 5: sparsity of the sample implies sparsity of the full D unless the sample was "specially tailored" which "would completely destroy utility" (p.9). The plant's dense tail is published precisely for utility (pane render freshness); tailoring it to hide nearest neighbours would hide the signal too. Broadcast to every mind is therefore broadcast to every auxiliary-knowledge holder.

- **The safe direction already in the tree is DP, not larger k (Narayanan §2 p.2 vs stats_boundary).** Narayanan notes k-anonymity "completely fails on high-dimensional datasets" [2] and Mere knowledge of the algorithm may break privacy [27]. The paper's scope explicitly excludes statistical databases where DP applies [26][1][3][7][5]. `stats_boundary.py` already carries the DP vocabulary for aggregated stats; the transfer is to name that the body path's `k_anonymity_*` metric is measurement only and that any histogram/sequence release derived from `DENSE_TAIL_BYTES` that leaves the trusted set `senses/health/body-research/inference-research` must be vetted as a DP release (epsilon/delta composition) or as a per-type consent revision — not as "k>=2 therefore safe."

None of this claims the plant has de-anonymized anyone, or that light alone is identifying, or that k-anonymity should be removed — Narayanan explicitly shows k-anonymity is insufficient, not that measuring k is harmful. The new work is naming, in the same file that carries the metric, that the metric does not prevent the attack its own citations predict, so a reviewer does not have to rediscover it.
